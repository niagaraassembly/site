AI training

Using AI well inside an organization is less about the model than about the setup around it: where it runs, what it can see, who can use it, and how you know it is behaving. This page lays out how teams, companies, secure and offline environments, and government and defence set that up, and what we want to build under Clark.

Aspirational. We do not currently offer AI courses, and nothing on this page can be booked. It is orientation, not legal or security advice, and the field changes fast; check the sources at the bottom.

Start with two questions

Almost every setup decision follows from these: where does the model run, and where does your data go? A public chat tool, an API in your own application, a model in your private cloud, a server in your own building, and a machine with no network at all are five different answers, and each brings its own infrastructure, controls and trade-offs.

Six ways organizations run AI

Setup and where it runsWho it suitsTrade-off
1. Hosted assistants
The provider’s cloud, through their apps.		 Individuals and teams who want to start quickly. Prompts and files leave your network. Check the provider’s terms on retention and training, and use business plans with admin controls.
2. API in your own tools
The provider’s cloud, called from your applications.		 Teams building AI into a product or workflow. You own the application, logging and access control around it.
3. Private cloud
Models offered inside your own cloud account and region.		 Companies that need data to stay in a tenancy and region they control. More set-up and cloud skills; residency and access are yours to manage.
4. Self-hosted, on-premises
Your own servers, running open-weight models.		 Firms with sensitive data or steady heavy use. You provide hardware, serving software, updates and expertise.
5. Air-gapped
A network with no outside connection.		 Defence contractors, health systems, financial and other regulated work. Everything, including models and updates, is brought in through controlled transfer.
6. Accredited government networks
Government-authorized environments, up to classified networks.		 Public sector and defence. Authorization, accreditation and oversight requirements dominate the design.

The building blocks

Whatever the setup, a working deployment is made of the same layers. Published reference architectures describe them as presentation, application logic, model integration and data, with monitoring and guardrails running across all of them.

LayerWhat it doesExamples
ModelsThe AI itself: hosted models, or open-weight models you run.Hosted frontier models; open-weight families such as Llama.
ServingRuns a model and answers requests.vLLM for shared servers; llama.cpp and Ollama for local and offline use.
Gateway and accessOne controlled front door: sign-in, roles, logging, rate limits.An AI gateway with single sign-on and role-based access.
Retrieval (RAG)Lets the model answer from your documents by looking them up first.Embedding models and a vector database such as Qdrant or Milvus.
Tools and agentsConnects a model to your systems and data so it can act.Model Context Protocol (MCP), AGENTS.md, agent frameworks such as goose.
Guardrails and evaluationFilters what goes in and out, and tests whether it behaves.Test sets, red-team exercises, input and output checks.
ObservabilityShows how the system is being used and whether it is healthy.Latency, throughput, token use, errors and GPU load.
HardwareThe compute underneath.GPUs for shared servers; CPUs and quantized models for small local setups.

1. Choose the usePick a specific task and judge what happens if the AI is wrong.
2. Choose the setupDecide where the model runs, based on how sensitive the data is.
3. Prepare data and accessClassify what the AI may see, and make sure it respects who is allowed to see what.
4. Build and connectWire in retrieval and tools through the gateway.
5. TestEvaluate against real examples, and try to break it before others do.
6. Roll outPublish clear rules of use and train the people who will use it, keeping a human in the loop where it matters.
7. Watch and improveMonitor use, quality and cost, and revisit as models and rules change.

Four situations in more detail

Teams and companies

  • Decide which tools are approved, and use business plans with sign-in and admin controls.
  • Set a data rule: what may and may not be pasted into an AI tool.
  • For internal knowledge, use retrieval with permissions respected, so people only get answers from documents they may see.
  • Log use, write a short usage policy, and train people in it.
  • Govern with a recognised framework: the NIST AI RMF for risk, and ISO/IEC 42001 if you need something you can be audited against.

Offline and secure workloads

  • Run an open-weight model locally with vLLM or llama.cpp; both can run with no outside connection, though vLLM needs careful network configuration for full isolation.
  • Keep embeddings and the vector database local too, so documents never leave the environment.
  • Use quantized models (formats such as GGUF, GPTQ, AWQ) to fit the hardware you have.
  • Bring models, software and updates in through controlled transfer, and check what you bring in.
  • Practitioner guides describe this pattern for defence contractors, health systems and financial firms.

Government and defence

  • U.S. impact levels. The Department of Defense authorizes cloud systems by impact level. IL5 covers controlled unclassified information and national security systems; IL6 covers classified data up to SECRET in dedicated classified environments.
  • In practice. In December 2025 the department launched GenAI.mil to put commercial AI tools, authorized at IL5, in front of about three million personnel. In 2026 it announced agreements to deploy AI on its classified networks.
  • Canada. The DND/CAF AI Strategy commits the defence team to being AI-enabled by 2030, with safe, responsible and ethical use. Federal departments are also covered by the Treasury Board’s Directive on Automated Decision-Making and its generative AI guidance.
  • Ontario. Bill 194 (the Enhancing Digital Security and Trust Act, 2024) sets AI requirements for public-sector entities, including public disclosure of use and human oversight.
  • The common thread is authorization, accountable oversight, and data staying inside approved boundaries.

Solo builders and small teams

  • Start with a model on your own workstation, using llama.cpp or Ollama, and keep your data on your machine.
  • Share it on a small server as use grows, then add retrieval over your own documents.
  • It is a natural companion to Linux skills, and to our Environments work: Theia now includes a framework for building AI-powered tools and IDEs.

Frameworks and standards

FrameworkWhoWhat it is
AI RMF 1.0 and the Generative AI ProfileNIST Voluntary guidance for managing AI risk, organized as Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. Released January 2023; the Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) followed in July 2024.
ISO/IEC 42001ISO/IEC A management-system standard for AI. An organization can be audited and certified against it.
