Robotics training

Robotics is where mechanics, electronics, software and safety meet, and it is where automation in this region’s shops is heading. This page maps the field, the standards bodies and credentials that already exist, and what we want to build under Clark.

Aspirational. We do not currently offer robotics courses, and nothing on this page can be booked. Where we describe what we would do, we say “would”. This page is orientation, not safety advice.

One field, many kinds of robot

“Robot” covers very different machines, and the rules that apply depend on which one you are dealing with.

KindWhat it isStandards that apply
Industrial robots and cells Arms doing welding, handling, machine tending or palletizing, usually inside a guarded work cell. ISO 10218-1 (the robot) and ISO 10218-2 (the application and cell); CSA Z434 in Canada.
Collaborative robots (cobots) Robots designed to work near or with people, without a full fence. Requirements now sit inside ISO 10218 (they were formerly ISO/TS 15066).
Mobile robots (AMRs, AGVs) Vehicles that move materials around a plant or warehouse. ISO 3691-4 for driverless industrial trucks; ANSI/A3 R15.08 in the U.S.
Personal care robots Robots that assist people, outside the factory. ISO 13482.
Open and research robots Built on open software such as ROS 2, on hobby and research hardware. No single safety standard; the rules follow the application.

Who writes the rules

BodyWhat it doesWhy it matters to you
ISO/TC 299 The international technical committee for robotics standards (it excludes toys and military applications). The source of ISO 10218, ISO 8373 (vocabulary), ISO 13482 and related standards.
CSA Group Publishes CSA Z434, Canada’s standard for industrial robots and robot systems. It adopts ISO 10218-1 and -2 with Canadian deviations. This is the version that counts for a shop in Ontario. The published edition adopts the 2011 ISO texts, so check whether a newer one has replaced it.
A3
Association for Advancing Automation		 The North American robotics and automation trade association. It coordinates U.S. robot safety standards and runs the Certified Robot Integrator program. Its safety resources and integrator certification are the closest thing to a common yardstick.
ROS-Industrial An open source project, supported by consortia in the Americas, Europe and Asia-Pacific, that brings ROS to industrial robots. Free training material, examples and interface standards.

The 2025 change worth knowing

ISO 10218-1 and -2 were revised in 2025 after nearly eight years of work. The update folded the collaborative-robot requirements of ISO/TS 15066 into the main standard, clarified requirements for functional safety, and added cybersecurity requirements that bear on robot safety. Part 1 is for robot manufacturers; Part 2 is for integrators and covers applications and robot cells. If you build a cell, Part 2 is your standard.

Credentials that exist

CredentialIssued byFor
Certified Robot Integrator A3 Companies. Involves a third-party audit, an on-site visit and hands-on testing of key staff, valid for two years.
Manufacturer certifications FANUC, KUKA, ABB, Yaskawa Programmers and technicians. Training centres in Canada cover controllers such as ABB IRC5, FANUC R-30iB, KUKA KRC4 and Yaskawa YRC1000. KUKA College issues a certificate on completion.
FANUC certification in college programs FANUC, delivered by schools Students. Mohawk College embeds FANUC certification in several engineering technology programs.
ROS 2 Industrial training ROS-Industrial (open materials) Anyone. Courses on ROS 2 basics, navigation and manipulation.
Trades and automation Skilled Trades Ontario, ISA Industrial Electrician and Millwright apprenticeships, ISA CCST and CAP. See Manufacturing training.

There is no single “robotics licence”. Skills are usually proven through a manufacturer’s program on a particular robot, backed by a trade or engineering background, while safety competence comes from knowing the standards above.

How a robot cell gets built

A useful way to see the whole field is to follow a cell from idea to running line. Each stage is a place where skills, and standards, come in.

StageWhat happens
1. DefineDecide the task, the parts, the throughput, and what could go wrong for the people nearby. Safety starts here, with a risk assessment.
2. ChoosePick the robot, tooling and layout to fit the task.
3. SafeguardDecide how people and the robot are kept apart or made safe together: guarding, sensors, or collaborative limits on speed and force.
4. IntegrateConnect the robot to the rest of the cell: controls, sensors, conveyors, machines.
5. Program and commissionTeach the motions, test them slowly, then bring the cell up to production.
6. Validate and hand overCheck the safeguarding works, document it, and train the people who will run it.
7. MaintainInspect, repair and update, and re-check safety whenever something changes.

Open robotics

ROS 2 (Robot Operating System 2) is an open source toolkit for building robot software, and the ROS-Industrial project extends it to industrial arms. It runs on affordable hardware such as a Raspberry Pi or an NVIDIA Jetson, so it is where students and startups can build real robots cheaply. It also connects to our Linux training. Whatever runs the robot, the safety requirements for a robot that works near people still come from the standards above.

What we would do

  • Introduce it. Evenings on what robots are, the kinds that matter to local shops, and the basics of robot safety.
  • Build with open tools. ROS 2 labs on small boards and a simple mobile robot, taught alongside Linux.
  • Show real cells. Tours and demos at shops that already use robots.
  • Point the way. Connect people to manufacturer programs and to college training such as Mohawk’s.
  • Teach what nobody certifies. Clark Courses on the everyday work around the robot: fixtures, end effectors, cell layout and troubleshooting.

What we need

  • Integrators and shops that use robots and would host a tour or a talk.
  • Programmers, technicians and safety professionals willing to teach.
  • Robots, cobots, mobile platforms and parts, lent or donated, for hands-on sessions.
  • Educators and students who want real projects.
