One neighbo(u)r to another

In 1842 a Buffalo grain merchant named Joseph Dart put his own credit behind a machine that had never worked anywhere. The engineering was not his. It belonged to Robert Dunbar, a Scottish mechanical engineer who had trained in Canada and come to Buffalo in 1834, and who knew how to hang a steam-driven chain of buckets off a warehouse wall and drop it into the hold of a lake schooner.

Another merchant, Mahlon Kingman, told Dart he would lose everything. Kingman was not a fool, and his argument was the sound one. Waterfront labour was cheap, could be hired by the day and let go by the day, and cost nothing at all when the ice closed the lake. Steam cost money whether or not a boat came in. It is the same argument you hear in any shop with a quote due Friday: what we run now works, and the new way has a capital cost we can see and a payback we cannot.

Dart's elevator moved 229,260 bushels its first season. Within fifteen years there were ten elevators on the Buffalo waterfront, twenty-seven by 1863, and by 1888 the harbour was handling more than 85 million bushels a year — past London, past Rotterdam, the largest grain port in the world, out of a place that had been a village of eighteen hundred people inside one lifetime. Kingman was not wrong about the price of labour. He was wrong about what a merchant and a machinist can do in one room, and about how fast the ground moves under an argument that has been correct for a hundred years.

What this region lost afterward usually gets called factories, and that is the wrong noun. Pisano and Shih supplied the right one: the industrial commons. It is what a place holds in common — process knowledge, tooling skill, the engineer who has already failed at this once and can tell you why, the shop three exits down that can run the job when your machine is down. No firm owns it. Firms draw on it and put back into it, mostly without noticing they are doing either.

Two things about a commons decide everything that follows. It is geographic: it lives in people, and people change employers inside a commuting radius, not across an ocean. And it cannot be bought. A country that has lost one cannot import a replacement, only reassemble one, in a specific place, out of the people who are still standing there. So the unit of repair is a region, not a nation — and this one runs from Brantford and Port Dover through Hamilton, east across Haldimand to Fort Erie and Niagara Falls, then over the river into Erie and Niagara counties and out to Rochester. The border is not the edge of the problem. It runs through the middle of it.

Nobody has fixed it yet, and we will start with our own end. Hamilton's Bayfront Industrial Area Strategy set out 47 actions. At the end of 2024, six were achieved, thirteen initiated, and twenty-eight had not been started; progress tracking had itself been paused in early 2024. That is the state of the plan for the largest block of serviced industrial land at the head of the lake.

Across the water, the year to January 2026 added about 4,700 private-sector jobs in Western New York and subtracted 2,000 in manufacturing. Both numbers are true at once, and only one of them gets a podium. The region is not idle. It is drifting out of the work that makes things.

Meanwhile the one input nobody can appropriate is running short. US manufacturing is projected to need 3.8 million people by 2033, with as many as 1.9 million of those jobs going unfilled. Ask firms what governs where reshored production actually lands and the quantity and quality of skilled workers comes out above tax treatment, above currency, above regulation, above tariffs, year after year. A government can move money in an afternoon. No government at any level can produce a millwright, a toolmaker or a controls technician in under several years, and none of them has any way to make the trade look worth entering to somebody deciding at seventeen. That work is regional. It gets done by employers, schools and shops that already know each other, or it does not get done.

Without it, what we get instead is the trap. Metal Craft Spinning and Stamping is a Canadian firm putting capacity into Niagara Falls, New York — roughly thirty kilometres from where it already operates — partly to sit on the correct side of a customs line. Nothing was invented. No capability entered the world that was not in it before. Work moved half an hour down the road, and two municipalities bid public money against each other for the privilege of hosting it. That is true at any rate anyone sets, and the rate has moved twice in the time it took to write this. Trade policy is weather. The commons is climate. You dress for the first and you build for the second.

Here is the part we keep forgetting: we have built the answer before, at both ends of this arc, and no government thought of it.

In 1839 the Hamilton & Gore Mechanics' Institute opened in rented rooms on King Street, paid for by subscription, so that anyone already working a trade could get at books, lectures and drawing instruction. In 1842, Dart and Dunbar. In 1885 Henry Lomb — of Bausch & Lomb — and a group of Rochester business owners founded a Mechanics Institute for the technical instruction of industrial workers; it merged with the Rochester Athenaeum in 1891 and was renamed the Rochester Institute of Technology in 1944. Trades and local business, three cities on one arc of water, arriving at the same answer inside fifty years with no ministry anywhere near it. Then we stopped, and the rooms emptied.

They are being reopened now, mostly by people who worked it out without anyone telling them it was a strategy.

At 2495 Main Street in Buffalo stands an Albert Kahn daylight factory that turned out more than 600,000 Model Ts from 1915, was leased in wartime to Hercules Motors and Bell Aircraft, then made Trico windshield wipers, then sat empty — 650,000 square feet of it — until Elgin Wolfe and a partner took it on in the early 1990s. The Tri-Main Center now holds small businesses, studios and Buffalo Arts Studio, and since 2025 it holds Bit Haven, a hackerspace where people teach each other to break into computers and to secure them. One building, one century, five industries, and the newest tenant in it is a room for handing on technical skill.

Four kilometres from where Dart's elevator stood, Rick Smith bought the derelict grain-elevator compound in 2006. Silo City's Lyceum runs hands-on teaching, its residencies work with The Foundry and others, and people have been living on the site since 2025. Those buildings are there because of what he and Dunbar built, and there are people inside them again, learning to make things. The Foundry, in east Buffalo, is a community makerspace with metal, wood, fibre and electronics shops, running workforce training, mentoring and business incubation. In Hamilton the 1900 Imperial Cotton mill is the Cotton Factory, full of studios and workshops.

None of those places has agreed to anything, and we claim none of them. We point at them because they settle a question: the form works, the building stock is standing, and the demand for it is real. What is missing is that they are not yet talking to each other as one region, and nobody has said out loud that it is all the same project.

What we propose is old and unglamorous. Shared tools, so a shop can get at a machine it cannot justify buying. Shared training, so an apprentice can learn on more than whatever their employer happens to own. Purchasing that stays local by decision instead of by accident. Capital raised from the people who live here. Every part of that has been done recently enough to check. Preston, in Lancashire, got its anchor institutions to look at where their spending actually went and then to change it: the share landing with local suppliers moved from 39 per cent to 79.2 per cent, redirecting roughly £200 million to firms in its own region, and the share of local jobs paying the real living wage went from 76 to 88 per cent. No new law was required. In Canada, Tapestry Community Capital has run community bond issues that have raised over $100 million from more than 4,500 ordinary investors, financing buildings and equipment in their own towns.

So here is the ask, and it is deliberately small. Put your name to this. Name, trade, town — that is the entire form. A machinist in Welland, a controls tech in Lackawanna, a retired millwright in Dunnville, a shop owner in Brantford, whoever runs purchasing at a hospital. We are not asking for money and we are not asking for a commitment. We are asking you to state that you are here and this is what you can do, because a list like that would be the first honest inventory this region has had of what it still knows how to make.

Then come to a meetup. The names matter, but the argument here is that things start when the people who make things and the people who buy things are in one room, so sooner or later the room has to actually happen.

What this is and is not, plainly. Niagara Assembly is not incorporated. It has raised nothing and holds nothing. There is no institution behind it, no staff, no office, and nobody is being paid. Nothing here is a promise, because we are in no position to make one. What exists is this statement, a roster of people who have put their names beside it, and some dates. If it ever grows into something that needs a legal form, that will happen in the open and it will be argued over first.

Kingman had the arithmetic and lost the argument anyway, because Dart had a machinist, a harbour, and a willingness to be wrong in public. We still have the harbour. We still have the shops on both shores and the people inside them. What we have lost is the habit of talking across the water, which is strange, because the nearest person who understands your problem is almost never in a capital city and is very often on the other side of the river. Start there. Add your name.
